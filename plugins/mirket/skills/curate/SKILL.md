# Curate

Ground the answer in sources. Choose the research branch for a question with no options to rank; use the comparison branch for a choice.

## Answer a research question

Define the question, the decision it informs and what evidence could answer it. Use primary sources for material claims and distinguish their observations from your inference. Link directly to the source and date time-sensitive evidence. Identify conflicting or missing evidence and what it prevents you from concluding. Return the shortest useful answer. When saving a reusable evidence note, keep it with the relevant plan or the designated deliverable; use `.work/temp/<task>/` for scratch research under the [workspace rules](../../references/workspace.md). Stop when the question is answered or its evidence gap is clear.

## Compare options

1. Identify the job, audience, constraints and decision criteria from the request and available context. Respect any named candidates or scope. Ask a focused question only when an unresolved requirement would materially change the options; continue research that does not depend on the answer.
2. Search current public sources for a broad candidate pool before narrowing it. Look beyond the most familiar alternatives using sources relevant to the category: official catalogs, project directories, public communities, independent comparisons, and discovery platforms such as Product Hunt where useful. Include credible lesser-known or emerging options when they fit the human's needs; do not include an option merely because it is obscure or trending.

## Check the evidence

3. For each serious candidate, inspect current sources that substantiate its fit and constraints. Use official documentation, repositories, release notes or vendor information for product and technical facts. Verify time-sensitive details such as compatibility, availability, pricing, licensing and maintenance when they matter to the choice.
4. Investigate public engagement, adoption and stability using signals appropriate to the category. For software, these may include release history, maintenance activity, issue and discussion activity, contributors, public usage reports or package downloads. For products, they may include public customer evidence, community discussion and relevant platform engagement. Record what the signals actually measure and their date or period; stars, votes, downloads, marketing claims or a recent release alone do not establish adoption, quality or stability.
5. Compare the candidates using the same relevant criteria: fit for the job, meaningful strengths and limitations, stability or maturity, adoption and public engagement, and any important costs or constraints. Use more than one type of evidence where practical. Distinguish current observations from older information, public claims from independently supported facts, and emerging options from established ones. Mark missing or inaccessible evidence as unknown; never invent metrics or imply independent verification where none exists.

## Present the options

6. Select a manageable range of genuinely relevant alternatives, including a less obvious candidate when the evidence supports one. Explain why each fits, its key tradeoff, and the public signals that support its assessment. Link directly to sources near the claims they support and date time-sensitive evidence. Distinguish observation from your judgment.
7. Give a recommendation when the evidence and requirements support one, explain the conditions under which another option would be better, and leave the decision with the human. If important criteria, sources or current research are unavailable, state the limitation and what it prevents you from determining; do not present an unverified shortlist as current research.

**Done:** The human can compare relevant options using current, attributed evidence about fit, stability, public engagement and adoption, with meaningful uncertainty and tradeoffs made clear.
