# Curate

Find and compare relevant options using current evidence. Scale research depth to the decision's stakes, uncertainty and cost. General research that does not involve an options comparison does not require this skill. Keep reusable evidence with its relevant plan or designated deliverable; use `.work/temp/<task>/` for scratch research under the [workspace rules](../../references/workspace.md).

## Compare options

1. Identify the job, audience, constraints and decision criteria from the request and available context. Include named candidates; ask a focused question when an unresolved requirement would materially change the options or decision, and continue research that does not depend on the answer.
2. Search current public sources for a broad candidate pool before narrowing it. Suggest additional relevant alternatives even when candidates are named, unless the human restricts the comparison. Look beyond familiar alternatives using category-relevant sources: official catalogs, project directories, public communities, independent comparisons, and discovery platforms such as Product Hunt where useful. Include credible lesser-known or emerging options when they fit the human's needs; do not include an option merely because it is obscure or trending.

## Check the evidence

3. For each serious candidate, inspect current sources that substantiate its fit and constraints. Use official documentation, repositories, release notes or vendor information for product and technical facts. Verify time-sensitive details such as compatibility, availability, pricing, licensing and maintenance when they matter to the choice.
4. Investigate public engagement, adoption and stability using signals appropriate to the category. For software, these may include release history, maintenance activity, issue and discussion activity, contributors, public usage reports or package downloads. For products, they may include public customer evidence, community discussion and relevant platform engagement. Record what the signals actually measure and their date or period; stars, votes, downloads, marketing claims or a recent release alone do not establish adoption, quality or stability.
5. Compare candidates using the same relevant criteria: fit for the job, meaningful strengths and limitations, stability or maturity, adoption and public engagement, and important costs or constraints. Prioritize fit and material risks; popularity is one signal, not a verdict. Use more than one type of evidence where practical. Distinguish current observations from older information, public claims from independently supported facts, and emerging options from established ones. Mark missing or inaccessible evidence as unknown; never invent metrics or imply independent verification where none exists.

## Present the options

6. Select a manageable range of genuinely relevant alternatives, including a less obvious candidate when the evidence supports one. Explain why each fits, its key tradeoff, and the public signals that support its assessment. Link directly to sources near the claims they support and date time-sensitive evidence. Distinguish observation from your judgment.
7. Give a recommendation when evidence and requirements support one, explain when another option would be better, and leave the decision with the human. When a material requirement is unresolved, offer conditional recommendations and ask about the decision that would change the choice. If current sources or important evidence are unavailable, state the limitation and what it prevents you from determining; offer a clearly provisional comparison when useful, without presenting it as current research.

**Done:** The human can compare relevant options using current, attributed evidence about fit, stability, public engagement and adoption, with meaningful uncertainty and tradeoffs made clear.
