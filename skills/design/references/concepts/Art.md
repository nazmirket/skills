# Art direction and illustration

Use for visual choices about subject, treatment, and material. Start with a concrete subject, medium, viewing condition, and what the audience should carry away. Find the acceptance and handoff in the relevant [format](../formats/README.md).

## Start from where it will be seen

Determine what the audience must understand or feel, where they see the work, the available size or time, and what they need to do next. Use the actual subject, material and wording to direct visual choices. A poster seen in passing, a slide projected full-screen and an image beside dense body text need different scale and detail.

Select one dominant element for the composition or frame. Sketch the eye path, important negative space, placement of copy and what can be cropped. Check how those decisions survive the smallest view that matters.

## Let the treatment carry meaning

Choose what must remain accurate, the degree of abstraction, and what color, light, framing or mark-making can explain. One purposeful analogy is usually stronger than several unrelated metaphors. Decoration can establish atmosphere without resembling evidence. For a series, repeat the visual logic and vary the composition where the subject needs it.

For example, an installation guide can use a simplified side view and directional marks to make orientation unmistakable. The corresponding announcement might use a closer crop and fuller texture for atmosphere. Preserve the product's recognizable geometry in both.

Use editable vectors for clear linework or forms that need to scale, and raster or photography when richer material serves the subject. Set essential lettering and labels separately if the image method can't render them reliably. Use [Illustration Design](../formats/IllustrationDesign.md) for artwork with placement or series requirements, [Canvas Design](../formats/CanvasDesign.md) for composed static work, and [Generative Art](../formats/GenerativeArt.md) when rules produce the artwork.
