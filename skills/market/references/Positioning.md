# Positioning

Position the offer against what a specific person would do otherwise, including manual work, an existing process, or doing nothing. Capture whether the audience chooses, uses, approves, or pays; these can be different people.

1. Describe the situation that triggers a search, what the person is trying to accomplish, their current workaround, and why switching is difficult. Ground each in direct observation, reported language, or a labeled inference.
2. Map the alternatives by decision criteria the audience cares about. Show which are genuinely comparable in the same context. Separate demonstrable differences from parity and attractive claims that lack proof.
3. Try distinct frames that would change the promise or who hears it. Compare fit with audience language, available proof, team capacity, and what the frame excludes. A claim about being “fastest” needs a defensible benchmark; usefulness for a specific recurring task can be demonstrated without that claim.
4. Produce a positioning statement: audience and trigger, category or frame, meaningful result, practical difference, proof, and who the offer does not fit. Keep that internal statement separate from public copy.

Use existing customer language to check who would recognize the offer, what they would compare it to and which claim needs proof. If fresh evidence would change the recommendation, propose those questions for a comprehension or first-choice conversation. Conduct outreach only when authorized. Record observed responses and what they change. Search supplies external evidence. Give proposed changes to the product or its target to Ship for a recommendation.

## Support a buying decision

For a sales or partner brief, name the role, purchase stage, current alternative and one question that reveals fit. Pair the buyer's objection with a demonstrable difference, its limitations and a useful next step. Verify comparison claims against dated material for the relevant plan or version. Record an unverified capability as unknown.

A buyer replacing a shared schedule asks whether two dispatchers can edit together. Give the seller a short demo case with both editing the same shift, the observed result and any plan restriction. If the buyer requires offline editing and that path is unsupported, record the gap for product discovery. Revisit the brief when the product, competitor terms or reasons for won and lost deals change.
