# Search

Go where the people working on the topic share what they are doing. Find evidence the human would miss in a generic overview. Search is read-only external research. Use [Adapt](../adapt/SKILL.md) for internal project facts and [Equip](../equip/SKILL.md) to decide whether a tool fits this project.

## Find the signal

1. **Set the question and the window.** Identify whose activity matters and what would count as a useful answer. For “trending” and “lately,” use an explicit date range and look for change within it. For history, follow earlier names and accounts back to the decisions or events that explain the present.
2. **Search where the work happens.** Use a broad search to find leads, then search the relevant places directly. Open actual threads, issue trackers, repository discussions, release histories, user forums, or other places where people exchange work and experience. For competitors, inspect the public product or a demo, documentation, and changelog for what it actually does; look at user discussions for how that plays out in practice. For history, read dated originals or archived copies. Pick sources to match the question; skip venues that add no useful perspective.
3. **Follow what the first results miss.** Search again using terms practitioners use, names and links found in the sources, or the workflows, workarounds, and objections people describe. Follow a promising lead to its source and look for a credible counterexample. Use tool access you already have; when a venue is unavailable, try an accessible source that can answer the same question and say where coverage is weaker. Keep queries free of private project details and leave postings and outreach outside this workflow.
4. **Check what the evidence supports.** Open the sources behind material claims. For a consequential claim, seek an independent signal; repeated coverage of the same announcement counts once. Distinguish what a product shows from what it promises, what one person reports from a broader pattern, and visibility from adoption. Use dated activity or comparable measures before calling something a trend. When the evidence is thin or conflicting, say what it does and does not establish.

## Return what changes the picture

Lead with the finding that answers the question. Link directly to the threads, artifacts, or original records that support it and give dates or a time window where they matter. Separate observations from interpretation and surface meaningful disagreements, coverage gaps, and less obvious discoveries. Stop when more searching is unlikely to change the answer or when the remaining gap is clear. If Search supports another skill, hand back the findings, source links, and limits so the owner can make the next decision.
