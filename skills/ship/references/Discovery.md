# Product discovery

Open with a decision and the cheapest evidence that could change it. Work backward from the assumption, not from a favorite method. Use existing data, support records, and approved research before asking for more.

| What might fail? | Useful evidence |
| --- | --- |
| People don't need the outcome enough | Recent examples of the problem, its frequency and consequence, or repeated effort spent on a workaround |
| People cannot complete the intended task | Observation or a realistic prototype with their own task and no coached path |
| The organization cannot support it | Direct evidence from the relevant owner about cost, policy, operations, channel or support constraints |
| The implementation cannot hold up | A bounded technical probe, reproduction or evidence returned by Architect or Test |

Ask for a recent specific instance: what triggered it, what the person did, what happened, and what it cost. Avoid selling the idea or accepting hypothetical intent as commitment. Record source, segment, date, contradictions, and who was missed. Collect no more personal information than authorized; distinguish verbatim observations, summaries, and inferences.

For a set of interviews or requests, group concrete episodes by the obstacle and outcome. Retain a pointer to each observation, the participant's role and the strongest exception. Count distinct people or accounts separately from mentions. Compare the observed obstacle with the reach of the affected workflow in product or support data. Include affected nonusers or people who abandoned the task when available.

For example, one dispatcher files six requests for bulk reassignment. That establishes repeated pain for one operator. Check a recent schedule change with another dispatcher and inspect how often several shifts move together. If the costly step is waiting for confirmation, test a confirmation change alongside bulk editing. Start with the assumption whose failure would remove the reason to invest.

Connect observed opportunities to the outcome and compare genuine alternatives. Before a trial, agree what would count as weak, strong or disconfirming evidence. Return the supported opportunity, remaining assumptions, proposed test and resulting decision. Bound prevalence claims to the population the evidence can represent.

## Deliver the outcome by hand

When a team can deliver the proposed value manually, use one normal customer decision to test demand before building. Specify what the customer supplies, who does each step, what the customer receives and when they can use it. Agree with the team on the observation that would justify another round and the staff time it can afford. Record the terms of the trial so a free repeat and a paid reorder retain their different meanings.

Suppose three repair shops each use a weekly parts count to place a supplier order. The proposed product would suggest what to reorder. For one ordering cycle, each shop supplies a permitted stock export and its reorder levels. An operator matches part numbers, flags stock below the agreed level and returns a draft order sheet before the cutoff. The buyer checks quantities and places their own order. Time the matching and exception handling separately.

Suppose the team will run a second cycle if at least two of the three buyers use the sheet for an order and request it again. Serving more shops would also require fulfillment within 20 minutes per shop. If two buyers do both but each sheet takes 35 minutes, repeat with those buyers. If 22 of those minutes went into the same part-number lookup, try a reusable mapping and time it again before expanding. If buyers ignore the sheet, find out how they placed their orders before investing in automation. A paid follow-up can test willingness to pay under terms the team has approved.
